Technique 02 — Reverse Brainstorming

Threat Analysis & Counter-Measures

How do you deliberately destroy this site? List every sabotage vector — then invert each one into a concrete design requirement.

SABOTAGE VECTOR — how to destroy this site → COUNTER-MEASURE — the requirement it reveals
Make the photographer completely invisible. Never mention Howard's name — not once, anywhere. → Credit Howard on every page — in the caption, the header, the footer. His name is structural, not decorative.
Apply a sterile modern UI: pure white, neutral sans-serif, flat design. Make it feel like a SaaS dashboard from 2024. → Commit fully to the period palette — olive drab, khaki, steel grey. Slab serif type that evokes 1944, not the current decade.
Give every ship the same generic one-liner: "Naval vessel photographed in the Pacific, circa WWII." Interchangeable. Forgettable. → Unique dossier card per ship: hull class, commission date, fate line. No two pages may feel identical.
Leave navigation dead-ends everywhere. Ships without content yet become 404 errors or completely blank pages. → Coming-soon placeholders on every ship slot from day one. The fleet index is always complete; only the content fills in over time.
Serve original uncompressed full-resolution scans with no optimization. Let the page take 40 seconds to load on a phone. → Optimized static site: compressed images, no server-side processing, fast load on every device. A static file wins here on all counts.
Design exclusively for 1440px desktop. Actively ignore every phone, tablet, and anything smaller than a monitor. → Mobile-first responsive layout. The full-width hero photograph must dominate on every screen — phone through desktop.
Never tell anyone when or where the photographs were taken. No date, no location, no era — strip all temporal context. → Establish Howard + San Francisco Bay + era c.1944–1946 in the opening paragraph of the homepage. State it on every ship page footer.
Scatter ships in random order with no index. Give visitors no map of the fleet and no way to know where they are. → Persistent fleet index in nav — one click from anywhere. Consistent, logical ordering across all twenty-one ships.
Present all history as certain undisputed fact with zero sourcing. Blend verified record with speculation freely. → Sourced history summaries with an explicit caveat distinguishing documented record from family memory or inference.
Omit an "About Howard" page entirely. Let him be a footnote in a single caption, then nothing. → About Howard nav slot present from launch — even if the page is coming-soon. His story must always be findable.
Design a completely unique bespoke layout for each of the twenty-one ships. No template, no consistency. Total visual chaos. → One consistent template applied identically to all twenty-one ships. The consistency signals a unified archive, not a scattered blog.
Technique 03 — Starbursting

The Six Interrogations

Rotating systematically through Who, What, Where, When, Why, and How — each axis forced the design space open from a different angle.

W
WHO — who is involved in and around this site?
  • Family members carrying Howard's memory and photographs forward
  • Maritime historians and dedicated WWII researchers
  • General public arriving via search or word of mouth
  • Howard himself — the witness whose viewpoint we are preserving
  • The sailors aboard each vessel captured in the frame
  • Future descendants who will need this context even more than we do
W
WHAT — what must every ship page contain?
  • Full-width hero photograph — the primary artifact, dominant above the fold
  • Dossier card: hull class, commissioned date, fate
  • "Where was it going?" narrative framing that gives the photo direction
  • Howard attribution caption anchored to the photograph
  • Sourced history summary with honest caveat
  • Prev/next navigation and back-to-fleet link
W
WHERE — where does essential context live on the site?
  • Homepage: Howard + Bay + era c.1944–1946 in the opening paragraph
  • Fleet index: persistent in nav, one click from anywhere
  • About Howard: own nav slot, always present and findable
  • Attribution caption: anchored directly to every photograph
  • Date range stated in the homepage subtitle and in ship page footers
W
WHEN — what temporal facts shape decisions?
  • The photographs were made c.1944–1946, wartime Pacific Fleet in SF Bay
  • Coming-soon pages matter immediately at launch — before all 21 ships have full content
  • The site should read correctly in 10, 20, or 50 years — no dated tech dependencies
  • Sourcing notes must state the date of research, not just the source title
W
WHY — why do specific design choices matter?
  • Attribution on every page transforms photos from generic to personal witness documents
  • Static site: reliability, speed, longevity — no backend to rot or be hacked
  • The fate line matters because ships were destroyed — the emotional weight must land
  • Consistent template signals a unified archive, not a scattered personal blog
  • Honest sourcing protects the archive's long-term credibility
H
HOW — how is the site structured and navigated?
  • 21 ships, each on its own dedicated page — one consistent template throughout
  • Prev/next on every ship page; back-to-fleet link always visible
  • Persistent fleet index in nav at all times
  • Mobile-first layout: hero photograph scales to fill any screen
  • Pure static HTML/CSS — no CMS, no database, no server-side code
  • All 21 placeholder pages live at launch, content fills in over time
Technique 04 — Utopia vs Dystopia

The Two Futures

Imagining the best and worst possible version of this site sharpened what truly matters — and what must be prevented at all cost.

✦ Utopia — the site we are building toward
  • Every ship has a complete dossier — class, commission date, fate — understood at a glance in seconds
  • Visitors feel they are opening Howard's personal photo album, not clicking through a website
  • The "where was it going?" framing turns each photograph from an image into a story with stakes
  • Howard is credited on every single page — his witness is permanent, recognized, findable
  • The fleet index gives the sense of the whole: twenty-one ships, one war, one man's camera
  • The design transports — dark, textured, period-appropriate — you feel 1944 the moment you arrive
  • Coming-soon slots mean the site feels complete even before all content is in place
  • Sourced history with honest caveats builds lasting trust — readers know exactly what is fact
✖ Dystopia — what we are designing against
  • Photos exist without context — nameless ships, unknown fates, Howard forgotten entirely
  • A modern sterile design makes 1944 photographs feel like clip art or stock imagery
  • Dead links and empty pages mean visitors leave without seeing the full fleet
  • No mobile support makes the site invisible to the majority of visitors who arrive on phones
  • Inconsistent templates make each ship page feel like a different, disconnected site
  • Generic captions strip the personal witness: "naval vessel, circa WWII"
  • History presented without sources — readers cannot separate documented fact from speculation
  • No About Howard page — the photographer vanishes entirely from his own archive
